Effects of Cycle Infrastructure on Copenhagen’s Air Quality

Sreenivas, Marquez, Kerns

The World Health Organization (WHO) found that more than 99% of the world population lives in areas where air quality is below their standards and that this discrepancy accounts for up to 4.2 million deaths per year. The culprits; Nitrogen Dioxide (NO2), Carbon Monoxide (CO), and particulate matter (PM), are created by everything from industry to household activities like cooking. NO2 and CO are both linked to causing respiratory ailments and aggravating lung-related diseases and conditions like asthma or bronchitis. Both gases are produced in urban areas, whether from heating apparatuses, engines that burn fossil fuels, or energy generation.

        Breathing NO2 in high concentrations results in airway irritation and headaches. The gas reacts with other Nitrogen Oxides (NOx) to form particulate matter and ozone, causing more problems for the health of the general public. Nitrogen Dioxide is especially harmful to young children and the elderly, as well as other people with preexisting lung conditions. In children, long exposure or high concentration of NO2 can stunt lung development and is a significant component in the development of asthma at early ages. Exposure to NO2 increases respiratory illnesses like coughing, wheezing, and abdominal pains for older people. NO2 aggravates pre-existing respiratory conditions in older patients, and in some cases is linked to death for those exposed for long periods.

        Nitrogen Dioxide mixes with oxygen, water, and other chemicals in the atmosphere to create acid rain, which can have detrimental effects to ecosystems, waterways, and human developments, like towns and cities. Forests and other green spaces are negatively affected by acid rain, destroying local environments, especially near urban centers and human developments. High levels of nitrogen oxides lead to hazy air, lowering visibility in both urban and rural zones. With the ability to stick to rain clouds and atmospheric condensation, Nitrogen oxides make their way to our oceans where the gas causes nutrient pollution in coastal waters and turn, killing aquatic animals like fish, crab, oysters, and other types of shellfish which humans eat.

Given the significant impact of nitrogen dioxide, with large amounts stemming from vehicle emissions, it is crucial to shift towards sustainable transportation methods. Riding cycles are an accessible form of transportation that benefits human and environmental health. In the city of Copenhagen, Denmark's capital city, an extensive system of cycle infrastructure has become the baseline for fighting NO2 emissions and promoting public health. They enabled and encouraged cycle culture, which resulted in more than 60% of Copenhagen’s residents commuting daily via cycle. This is supported by over 400 kilometers of protected bike lanes. Cycling culture and bike lane infrastructure positions Copenhagen as a living laboratory for studying the cause and effects of cycling as a transportation mechanism, as well as the environmental health and the impact on the residents of such communities.

Cities with larger populations often rely on increased urbanization and infrastructure to accommodate the sheer volume of citizens who frequently struggle with rising air pollution, climate change, and public health challenges. This results in a pressing need to mitigate urban air pollution. Transportation accounts for approximately 25% of global CO2 emissions with road vehicles contributing to most of those emissions. In contrast, Copenhagen reduced their reliance on fossil fuels by investing in renewable energy sources and promoting public transportation, effectively lowering the emissions of pollutants like NO2, carbon dioxide, and carbon monoxide.

Anecdotal evidence and localized studies suggest a correlation between improved bike lane infrastructure and reduced air pollution, but the precise magnitude of this impact and spatial distribution of these effects requires additional exploration. An in-depth study can utilize advanced tools like satellite spatial and temporal data. Such tools can present us with quantifiable impacts of Copenhagen’s bike-centric transportation model. They can present a compelling case for the influence of increased bicycle use on air quality, and consequently the improvement in environmental and population health.

Our article aims to integrate remote sensing data with ground-level air quality measurements and health records across Copenhagen. This area of study makes it possible to quantify and link the increased use of bike lanes for transportation to measurable air quality outcomes. Satellite data offers spatial and temporal coverage to visualize air pollution changes pre- and post-bike lane construction. For this report, we will use the following datasets.

Sentinel-5P TROPOMI

TROPospheric Monitoring Instrument (TROPOMI) sensors aboard Sentinel 5P satellite record data for measuring concentration of trace gases and the prevalence of aerosols in the atmosphere (Sentinel-5P Datasets in Earth Engine | Earth Engine Data Catalog). TROPOMI features a spatial resolution of 7 km x 3.5 km in addition to a daily temporal resolution which allows for the high precision measuring of critical air pollutants such as nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), carbon monoxide (CO), and aerosol optical depth (AOD). This dataset is available from 2018, and cannot be used to do any analysis using historical data. Using TROPOMI data, this report will evaluate the impact of cycling culture and bike lane infrastructure on the environmental health of Copenhagen.

Investment in bike lane construction in Copenhagen has expanded significantly since the 1980s. Bicycle traffic accounts for a significant proportion of Copenhagen traffic – comparable to public transport and private cars (City of Copenhagen, Cycle Policy 2000-2012). TROPOMI data available from 2018 onwards tracks the air quality trends associated with the post bike lane expansion time frame, and can help analyze changes in NO₂ concentration, an air pollutant which is a key emission from automobiles. Any observed reduction in NO2 concentration levels over central Copenhagen will determine how the change can be attributed to 450 kilometer cycle tracks within the city.

TROPOMI can detect and map pollutant concentrations with high sensitivity and precision, including detecting NO₂ down to 0.00004 mol/m², CO at a measurement of 0.001 mol/m², and AOD being used as a particulate matter proxy. With a high spatial resolution (7 km x 3.5 km), it enables detection of spatial gradients in NO₂ concentrations, we can observe this for lower concentrations of nitrogen dioxide along high-cycling corridors like Norrebrogade, used daily by approximately 40,000 cyclists. This capability is well-documented in Griffin et al. (2019), "High resolution mapping of nitrogen dioxide with TROPOMI: First results and validation over the Canadian oil sands" (Geophysical Research Letters, 46(2), 1049-1060), which highlights TROPOMI’s ability to resolve fine-scale pollution patterns. This methodology applies to the area of interest for this report - Copenhagen. Time-series graphs to visualize annual means will be created using this data. Statistical reductions in concentration of atmospheric pollutants will be overlaid with bike lane rider density maps to visualize the relationship between cleaner air and bike lane infrastructure.

TROPOMI provides data starting in 2018, and this is much later than the bike lane construction and usage started in the 1980s. This limitation in temporal data scope is a setback as this dataset cannot show the evidence to compare the air quality before and after the bike lane construction. To assert historical impact of investments in bike lane infrastructure, TROPOMI must be paired with historical proxies (e.g., Landsat AOD, ground archives), highlighting the need for integrated, multi-source approaches to close these evidentiary gaps.

FAO Global Administration Unit Layers

FAO Global Administrative Unit Layers (GAUL) 2015, Second-Level Administrative Units dataset provides a standardized global spatial database of administrative boundaries at various scales, including second-level units like districts and municipalities. This dataset is highly valuable for studying the impact of bike lane infrastructure investments on environmental and population health in Copenhagen. GAUL supports integrating air pollution measurement datasets like MODIS AOD with 1 km resolution or TROPOMI NO2 with 7 km x 3.5 km spatial resolution to calculate district level air pollution metrics. This dataset also integrates with WorldPop at 100m spatial resolution to estimate the number of residents exposed to improved air quality. However, this dataset too lacks pre-2015 temporal depth.

How do these data sources provide evidence beyond what we already know?

Sentinel-5P TROPOMI data is only available from 2018. However, there is a need for pre-2018 data for analysis. Effectively, this study will use GIS and satellite data from Sentinel-5P TROPOMI at 7 km resolution, supplemented by ground sensors to map pollution gradients and correlate them with bike lane density.

It is crucial to consider the temporal patterns influencing air quality data to effectively analyze pollution trends and their relationship with bike lane density. Focusing specifically on the summer months is of particular interest, as weather conditions tend to be more stable during this time, which can lead to increased outdoor activities. The warmer temperatures and longer daylight hours encourage more people to bike, providing a significant opportunity to examine the correlation between the higher presence of cyclists and variations in air quality.

Figure 2: Map of Copenhagen measuring the NO2 Tropospheric Column in the summer of June 1 - August 31 in 2018, 2020, 2022, 2024.

The decision to focus on data collected during the summer months, specifically from June 1 to August 31, is intentional. The summer typically features more stable weather patterns with less atmospheric turbulence, providing a clearer insight into pollution trends. Summer is a period of increased outdoor activity, travel, and tourism, in which biking is highly encouraged. Visualizing Copenhagen from 2018 to 2024 shows a clear trend of decreasing nitrogen dioxide (NO2). In 2018, NO2 concentrations were noticeably higher, especially in central urban areas, while by 2024, these levels had reduced significantly [Figure 2]. The yellow regions on the map indicate areas with approximately 0.00001 mol/m² of NO2, representing a concerning pollution level. In contrast, the green areas reflect regions with around 0.000005 mol/m², showcasing a much healthier air quality. The decline indicates a notable improvement in air quality over the years regarding the NO2 concentrations.

Figure 3: Bar graph illustrating the monthly average of NO2, AQI, PM2.5, and CO trends from June to August 2020 to 2024.

The trend graph in Figure 3 shows a drop in NO2 levels around 2020. The particulate matter (PM2.5) levels increase slightly after 2023. As the carbon monoxide trend data was retrieved in 2023, it is shown that there is a trend in levels as in the months of June, the CO level average is above 75 but drops in July then increasingly passes 100 ppb in the month of August for the years of 2023 and 2024. This trend correlates with Copenhagen’s ongoing investment in bike infrastructure. As one of the world’s most bike-friendly cities, Copenhagen has expanded its network of dedicated bike lanes, bike highways, and traffic calming measures designed to prioritize cyclists. These improvements have encouraged more residents to adopt cycling as their main mode of transportation, reducing their need to use motor vehicles. The decrease in NO2 levels suggests that this shift has had a meaningful impact, as fewer cars on the road directly result in lower emissions from combustion engines.

Figure 4: Monthly Average Trends graph in 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, and 2024

Figure 4 illustrates a graph for the monthly average trends of air quality indicators, including NO2 (ppb), AQI (Air Quality Index), PM2.5 (µg/m³), and CO (ppb) over a span of five years from early 2020 to present 2025. The NO2 levels (represented in blue) remain relatively stable throughout the observed period, with slight decreases post-2020, aligning with a potential reduction in vehicular emissions due to the effects of COVID-19. The AQI (in red) follows a consistent pattern, indicating stable air quality trends despite fluctuations in individual pollutant levels. PM2.5 concentrations (in yellow) monthly average remains low, and the World Health Organization states the “recommended maximum levels for NO2 [is] at 25 μg/m3 for each 24-hour mean, and 10 μg/m3 for the annual mean level (WHO).

The data found in this study revealed distinct trends in pollutant behavior, which are correlated to policy decisions related to cycling, a population’s choices in transportation, and access to environmentally friendly modes of transportation. Copenhagen excels as a model for other cities to follow, the ball has already begun rolling in other Danish towns like Odense and Åarhus, where officials are petitioning for grants to boast their cycle infrastructure. By creating a culture of cyclists, Copenhagen has ensured healthy citizens for years to come. With continued attention to municipal climate goals, the city is on a very green path heading into the future. Copenhagen’s dedication to cycle infrastructure will protect not only the city’s environmental health, but the health of residents young and old as more citizens turn to cycles or other green transport options, fewer personal vehicles will be on the roads, creating NO2 and CO at much lower rates. Cycle infrastructure remains an easy and effective way to reduce the production of harmful pollutants while promoting equity and public health for all inhabitants.
