The Universality of Human Rights

The idea of universal human rights was conceived as a response to the human suffering caused by war. In the aftermath of the Second World War's unprecedented human atrocities and resulting loss of millions of lives from holocaust, the newly formed United Nations sought to create universal safeguards to prevent such horrors from repeating again. Members of the Commission on Human Rights, chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR).  Adopted by the third United Nations General Assembly in 1948, UDHR is the foundational document proclaiming human rights in “recognition of the inherent dignity and of the equal and inalienable rights of all members of the human family” (UDHR Preamble, 1948).  

“Human rights are rights we have simply because we exist as human beings - they are not granted by any state” (What are Human Rights? | OHCHR). Presented in form of 30 Articles in the UDHR, these rights protect civil liberties like freedom from torture and political participation, and socio-economic entitlements such as education and healthcare. These rights belong to all humans regardless of nationality, ethnic origin, sex, language, religion, or any other factor; they apply equally to every person everywhere, without exception. UDHR asserts these rights are universal, inalienable and indivisible. However, the universality of human rights proclaimed by this Declaration is not accepted by all nations. UDHR faces criticism for allegedly imposing “Western” values on other nations globally. This essay examines this criticism, and presents my argument concluding this criticism is not justified.

The Declaration was drafted by a committee formed by the UN Commission on Human Rights. The drafting committee included members from China, Lebanon, France, US and countries from other regions. The process was intentionally inclusive, with representatives from different legal and cultural experiences. One of the contributors to this document was Hansa Mehta, a representative from India. She was credited with changing the wording in Article 1 to “All human beings are equal in dignity and rights…” (Lecture Video: UDHR). For the first time, the universality of fundamental human rights was drafted by a diverse group of people, declaring that these rights belong to all humans, everywhere.

While 8 UN member-states abstained from voting, none of the countries voted no on the Declaration. 48 countries voted yes. This indicates there are disagreements among member nations with some of the rights as proclaimed in UDHR. One of the valid criticisms is, although the drafting committee was composed of representatives from different parts of the world, the drafting process was strongly influenced by “Western”’ nations. This power dynamic most likely shaped the declaration’s philosophical foundations without considering the cultural differences that exist between the Western countries and the other parts of the world. The Communist countries expressed their disagreements around the view that freedom of opinion and expression protected by Article 19 that emphasized individual autonomy to a right to freedom of opinion (UDHR, Article 19, 1948) over communal rights for the collective well-being, because they opposed providing individual rights to freedom of opinion and expression to the Nazis. Representatives from Saudi Arabia abstained because of the wording in Articles 16 and 18, that can be summed up as objections based on religion, while other Islamic countries like Iran, Pakistan and Syria voted in favor of the Declaration. Even if these countries did not vote no, these disagreements exist proving that the universality of UDHR is nuanced. Despite aspirations to universality, the UDHR’s legacy remains entangled with a notion of cultural imperialism, and in its enforcement, there are cases that align with Western geopolitical interests.

It’s important to consider that “the Declaration simply declares certain values to be human rights”1 (What Human Rights Mean, Charles Beitz), UDHR does not have binding authority, it provides a common moral language, and does not enforce a rigid blueprint. Every society and culture can pursue their own definition of human rights, as long as they don’t encroach upon the rights of other individuals who exist within that culture. UDHR, as a foundational document, enables people in different cultures and different parts of the world to find specific parameters within their cultural, legal and social framework to define and protect their rights aligned with the fundamental values enshrined in the Declaration. It’s also important to note that values like tolerance and civic participation in democracy are not unique to the West2 (Universal Truths: Human Rights and the Westernizing Illusion, Amartya Sen). Article 5 prohibits torture, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment (UDHR, Article 5, 1948), which aligns with values system of the East. This adaptability in the Declaration counters the narratives of cultural imperialism, and the Declaration’s flexibility enables culturally attuned adoption.

The African Commission on Human and Peoples’ Rights (ACHPR) drafted a regional human rights treaty that adopted the fundamental rights declared in the UDHR to African contexts. Drafted by the Organization of African Unity (OAU), this regional treaty reinterprets UDHR principles through African philosophical frameworks like Ubuntu ("a person is a person through others") and balances individual rights with community responsibilities (Human rights and cultural perspectives | UNESCO). For instance, Article 29 of the African Charter states: "The individual shall also have the duty... to preserve and strengthen positive African cultural values." (Article 29, The African Charter). This example counters cultural imperialism claims by illustrating how African nations reinterpreted the UDHR framework to align with their cultural values, thus empowering local communities to define human rights through their own traditions and experiences. Despite its origins and influences, UDHR has proven to be a flexible framework that allows for cultural interpretations, making it a truly global standard and therefore the universality it offers is applicable.

The process of drafting the UDHR, although imbalanced, still incorporated inputs from non-Western representatives like Charles Malik from Lebanon, P.C.Chang from China, and Hansa Mehta from India. The text in the final version of the Declaration emerged from multiple rounds of negotiations resulting in shaping up of Articles 22 to 27 focused on defining socio-economic rights as envisaged by the Soviet and Latin American representatives. UDHR is still not a perfect document, it is unevenly applied with double standards in calling attention to human rights violations based on international politics and political affiliations. Such infractions should be attributed to the actors tasked with implementation, but the Declaration itself does not deserve criticism for pushing a Western agenda.

In my opinion, lack of substantive evidence weakens the cultural imperialism thesis. And, cultural relativism may risk undermining accountability for human rights violations. UDHR is a flawed, yet vital framework that universally protects the rights of all humans, but is not a rigid and radical imposition enforcing “Western” values on the entire world. Criticism of Western bias should lead to reform, and not to a rejection of the entire Declaration. Rights to life, dignity, protection from torture, freedom from slavery are globally resonant. UDHR serves as “a moral foundation for advocacy and implementation of human rights laws globally”. Its global adoption and endurance suggest a deeper human resonance than mere imposition of Western influence.
