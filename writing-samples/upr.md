## The Universal Periodic Review: Progress or Performative?
### A Critical Analysis of India’s Fourth Cycle

> 944 words, 3-4 pages

The Universal Periodic Review (UPR) is a process that reviews the human rights records of UN Member States. Created in 2006 after forming the Human Rights Council, it ensures all 193 UN countries undergo peer reviews every four-and-half years, and helps states solve human rights challenges globally through cooperation and dialog. With “an ultimate goal to improve the human rights situation in every country” (Basic facts about the UPR &#124; OHCHR) through review and recommendations, has the UPR actually improved human rights worldwide, or is it just a symbolic ritual without real change? India’s fourth UPR cycle, conducted in 2022, will be studied to assess the effectiveness of the UPR process. Drawing on India’s national report, the Working Group’s recommendations, the interactive dialog, and critical theoretical frameworks like human rights law, international relations, sovereignty and power politics, this essay will analyze and conclude that the UPR is more than performative but stops short of transformative change, it fosters incremental progress but struggles to address entrenched abuses, and its value is in creating spaces for dialog, not in enforcing rights.  

At the 4th UPR cycle, India cast itself as “firmly committed to the promotion and protection of human rights” (A/HRC/WG.6/41/IND/1, 2022, p.2) both at home and in its international engagements, and reported broad progress in human rights protections under the Constitution’s commitment to equality and non-discrimination. While acknowledging ongoing challenges, the report positions India as actively pursuing inclusive development and strengthening rights for women and marginalized groups through a combination of constitutional safeguards, legislative action, targeted policies, and international engagement. Special emphasis is placed on the elimination of discrimination against women, citing amendments that promoted workforce participation, improved reproductive rights, increased maternity benefits, improved educational access and gender parity, and increased women’s representation in political and community life.  

During the interactive dialog, 130 delegations made statements (A/HRC/52/11, 2023, p.3). India received 339 recommendations (A/HRC/52/11, 2023, p.9-26), ranging from poverty reduction to the abolition of certain security laws. India “Supported” 221 of the 339 recommendations, and “Noted” 111 recommendations (A/HRC/52/11/Add.1, 2023, p.2-11). Supported recommendations aligned closely with India’s own stated priorities; economic, social and cultural rights, poverty alleviation, women’s empowerment, protections for children, minorities and persons with disabilities, and incremental improvements in justice and governance institutions. However, recommendations noted by India called for ratifying international treaties not yet signed or ratified by India, for example, the Convention against Torture, repealing or amending sensitive national laws, imposing a moratorium or abolishing the death penalty, addressing regional or contested political issues (especially Kashmir). Recommendations perceived to conflict with the Constitution of India, “national context,” or ongoing domestic processes were noted. Sovereignty and security featured prominently as rationale for refusing or deferring sensitive reforms.  

Strategic partners like Russia, and other developing economies including Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Ghana, Ethiopia, Nigeria, Kuwait, UAE, Oman, Mauritius, and Seychelles, commended India's development agenda, social programs, poverty alleviation and COVID-19 response, reflecting solidarity or strong diplomatic/economic ties. Conversely, critical interventions mainly came from Western European and North American states, Nordic countries, some Latin American states, South Korea, Japan, and Australia. These nations raised concerns about civil liberties, freedom of expression, religious freedom, women's rights, and the protection of minorities. Pakistan and Turkey delivered particularly strong criticisms regarding Kashmir and minority rights, driven by historical and regional tensions.  

India’s 2022 UPR engagement shows clear evidence of real progress in human rights  protections, blended with cautious, selective responsiveness on politically sensitive human rights concerns. The session’s tone was marked by formal statements, adherence to diplomatic protocol, and a clear effort by all participants to maintain a respectful, orderly environment. Interventions by member states frequently included both commendations for India’s economic and social achievements and diplomatic expressions of concern regarding human rights issues. Even pointed criticisms on issues such as civil society restrictions, religious freedom, and legal impunity were phrased using cautious and non-confrontational language. Responses from the Indian delegation mirrored this approach: upholding national sovereignty and sovereignty-sensitive issues with restraint, while reaffirming the robust legal and institutional frameworks that underpin the protection of human rights in India. This thoughtfully measured tone is performative, however it also illustrates the fundamental goal of the UPR process to promote constructive discussion of human rights in this context rather than in an adversarial or punitive setting. This “diplomatic performance” helps maintain international dialog and disincentivize the worst regression or “human rights backsliding” by member states. Therefore, the UPR’s effectiveness lies in securing incremental improvements where political space exists, and in maintaining global attention on unresolved issues. India’s review demonstrates the duality of genuine advancement where feasible, alongside strategic deflection where sovereignty and national interests predominate.  

In conclusion, the UPR is more than just a diplomatic performance; it plays a real, if limited, role in influencing state behavior. The review process compels UN Member States to publicly engage with their human rights records, subject themselves to peer and public scrutiny, and formalize certain policy intentions, fostering transparency and accountability. However, the UPR does not operate as a robust engine for transformative rights reform. Its design prioritizes dialog and mutual encouragement over enforcement, with state sovereignty ultimately setting the boundaries for change. India’s 2022 review embodies both sides: real advancement in welfare and legal frameworks, coupled with strategic resistance to the most vital issues of liberty, nondiscrimination, and security-sector reform. The UPR process is more than performative, but less than transformative – A process that reflects and reinforces the world as it is, not as how the human rights idealists might wish it to be. Its lasting contribution is to keep human rights on the global agenda, even if some of the most difficult truths remain “Noted” and unresolved for now.
